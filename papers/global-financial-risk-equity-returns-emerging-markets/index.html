Global Financial Risk, Equity Returns and Economic Activity in Emerging Countries

Jaroslav Horvath (University of New Hampshire); Guanyi Yang (Colorado College)
Published article · Oxford Bulletin of Economics and Statistics, Vol. 86, No. 3, pp. 672–689. DOI 10.1111/obes.12595
Theme: Emerging markets: informality and global financial shocks

Global financial risk shocks reach emerging economies mainly through equity markets, not sovereign spreads: when the response of equity returns to the VIX is shut down, the share of output variance explained by global financial risk falls from about 10 percent to 2 percent, while shutting down the spread response changes almost nothing.

Abstract

International financial integration exposes countries to external shocks. This paper identifies the impact and transmission of global financial risk (GFR) shocks to emerging market economies (EMEs). Heightened GFR significantly raises EME borrowing costs and lowers equity returns, reducing domestic economic activity. We document a novel transmission channel of GFR shocks to EMEs via international capital flows. Countries experiencing larger capital inflows are more affected by GFR fluctuations. Exploring the transmission through capital flows, GFR shocks affect EMEs mainly through their effect on equity returns, instead of country spreads. We show that equity returns contain more information about EME macroeconomic fluctuations than sovereign and corporate bond spreads.

What this paper shows

  • A one-standard-deviation increase in global financial risk (VIX) lowers output growth by over 0.4 percentage points and investment growth by over 0.8 points, with troughs about five quarters after the shock, alongside capital outflows, higher country spreads and falling equity prices.
  • Global financial risk explains about 10 percent of output variance, 5 percent of investment variance, 7 percent of country-spread variance and over 15 percent of equity-return variance.
  • Countries with high exposure to capital inflows (Brazil, Chile, Mexico, the Philippines, South Africa, Thailand) see global financial risk explain over 15 percent of output variance, roughly double the share in low-exposure countries.
  • Counterfactuals: muting the equity-return response cuts the global-risk share of output variance from about 10 to 2 percent and of investment variance from 5 to near zero; muting the country-spread response leaves both essentially unchanged.
  • Equity-return innovations account for about 25 percent of output and investment variance; sovereign spread innovations account for a negligible share, and equity returns dominate corporate spreads (EFI and CEMBI) as well.

What it does not show

  • Twelve emerging economies, 1999Q1–2019Q4 (Indonesia from 2004Q2, Thailand to 2006Q1); the sample ends before the pandemic.
  • Identification is a recursive panel SVAR with global financial risk ordered as an exogenous block and equity returns ordered last; capital flows are measured vis-à-vis the United States (TIC data), with a robustness check using global flows.
  • The collateral-constraint model (Mendoza and Smith 2006) is used to interpret the mechanism, not estimated.

Context and definitions

  • Global financial risk is proxied by the VIX in the published version and by the U.S. Baa spread in robustness checks; the 2022 working paper version reversed those roles.
  • Country spreads are J.P. Morgan EMBIG; corporate spreads are the EFI of Caballero, Fernández and Park (2019) and J.P. Morgan’s CEMBI.

Data, method, contribution

Data
IMF International Financial Statistics (output, investment, trade balance); J.P. Morgan EMBIG spreads via World Bank GEM; Global Financial Data stock indexes; U.S. Treasury TIC capital flows; VIX and Baa spread from FRED. Quarterly, 1999Q1–2019Q4.
Method
Panel structural VAR (least-squares dummy variable estimation, one lag, recursive identification) with output, investment, trade balance, global financial risk, capital flows, country spread and equity returns; impulse responses, variance decompositions and counterfactuals that mute specific transmission channels.
What is new
Evidence that the international equity channel, not bond spreads, is the main conduit of global financial risk to emerging economies, and that equity returns carry information about emerging-market activity beyond sovereign and corporate spreads.

Who this is for

  • International macroeconomists and finance researchers on emerging-market business cycles
  • Economists working on the global financial cycle and VIX spillovers
  • Emerging-market central bank research departments
  • Asset managers and analysts following EM equities and capital flows

Questions this paper answers

How do VIX shocks affect emerging economies?

A one-standard-deviation rise in the VIX lowers emerging-market output growth by over 0.4 percentage points and investment growth by over 0.8 points within about five quarters, triggers capital outflows, raises sovereign spreads and lowers equity prices.

Do stock returns or bond spreads matter more for emerging-market business cycles?

Stock returns. Equity-return innovations explain about a quarter of output and investment variance; sovereign and corporate spread innovations explain little, and the transmission of global financial risk runs through equity prices rather than spreads.
