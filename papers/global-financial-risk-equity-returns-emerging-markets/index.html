Global Financial Risk, Equity Returns and Economic Activity in Emerging Countries

Jaroslav Horvath, Guanyi Yang

Oxford Bulletin of Economics and Statistics, Vol. 86, No. 3, pp. 672–689

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article linked here. Earlier working-paper versions are listed separately below.

Abstract

International financial integration exposes countries to external shocks. This paper identifies the impact and transmission of global financial risk (GFR) shocks to emerging market economies (EMEs). Heightened GFR significantly raises EME borrowing costs and lowers equity returns, reducing domestic economic activity. We document a novel transmission channel of GFR shocks to EMEs via international capital flows. Countries experiencing larger capital inflows are more affected by GFR fluctuations. Exploring the transmission through capital flows, GFR shocks affect EMEs mainly through their effect on equity returns, instead of country spreads. We show that equity returns contain more information about EME macroeconomic fluctuations than sovereign and corporate bond spreads.

Main findings

  • A one-standard-deviation adverse global-risk innovation reduces annual output growth by over 0.4 percentage points and annual investment growth by over 0.8 points at the trough, about five quarters after the shock (Figure 2).
  • In the baseline 20-quarter forecast-error variance decomposition, global risk contributes about 10 percent of output-growth variance, close to 5 percent of investment-growth variance, more than 7 percent of country-spread variance and over 15 percent of equity-return variance (Figure 3).
  • In countries with above-median average net US capital inflows relative to GDP, global risk accounts for over 15 percent of output-growth forecast-error variance, roughly twice the share in the lower-exposure group (Figure 4).
  • Removing the direct response of equity returns to global risk reduces its output-growth variance share from about 10 to 2 percent and its investment-growth share from 5 to near zero. Removing the country-spread response leaves these shares almost unchanged (Figures 5 and 6).
  • Equity-return innovations account for about a quarter of output- and investment-growth forecast-error variance in this specification. Sovereign-spread innovations contribute little, and equity innovations also explain more than corporate-spread innovations in the reduced sample (Figures 7 and 8).

Data and approach

Data
IMF International Financial Statistics (output, investment, trade balance); J.P. Morgan EMBIG spreads via World Bank GEM; Global Financial Data stock indexes; U.S. Treasury TIC capital flows; VIX and Baa spread from FRED. Quarterly, 1999Q1–2019Q4.
Method
Panel structural VAR (least-squares dummy variable estimation, one lag, recursive identification) with output, investment, trade balance, global financial risk, capital flows, country spread and equity returns; impulse responses, variance decompositions and counterfactuals that mute specific transmission channels.
Contribution
Compares equity and borrowing-spread transmission channels in emerging-market business cycles, including international capital flows in the estimated panel SVAR.

Scope and interpretation

  • Twelve emerging economies, 1999Q1–2019Q4 (Indonesia from 2004Q2, Thailand to 2006Q1); the sample ends before the pandemic.
  • Identification is a recursive panel SVAR with global financial risk ordered as an exogenous block and equity returns ordered last; capital flows are measured vis-à-vis the United States (TIC data), with a robustness check using global flows.
  • The collateral-constraint model (Mendoza and Smith 2006) is used to interpret the mechanism, not estimated.

Context

  • The published baseline uses the VIX as the global-risk proxy; a robustness exercise uses the US Baa credit spread.
  • Country spreads are J.P. Morgan EMBIG; corporate spreads are the EFI of Caballero, Fernández and Park (2019) and J.P. Morgan’s CEMBI.

Further questions

How do global financial-risk shocks reach emerging economies?

The panel SVAR estimates capital outflows, lower equity returns, higher borrowing spreads and weaker output and investment growth after an adverse global-risk innovation. Their interpretation depends on the recursive identification and small-open-economy assumptions.

What does the equity-channel counterfactual show?

Within the estimated model, removing equity returns' direct response to global risk sharply reduces the share of output-growth forecast-error variance attributed to global risk. The corresponding sovereign-spread counterfactual changes little. This is a channel decomposition, not an observed policy experiment.
