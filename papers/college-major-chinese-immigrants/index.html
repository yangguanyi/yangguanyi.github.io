The Effect of College Major on Labor Market Outcomes of Chinese Immigrants: An Examination of Undergraduate Major Choices and Their Impact on Employment and Earnings

Guanyi Yang

Journal of the Knowledge Economy, Vol. 6, No. 4, pp. 1138–1162 (published online 28 March 2014)

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article linked here. Earlier working-paper versions are listed separately below.

Abstract

Education is a crucial factor that determines labor market outcomes, especially for immigrants. This paper specifically examines the undergraduate major choice for Chinese immigrants and its relationship to their labor market outcome. Compared to other Asian groups and the mainstream society, Chinese immigrants are uniquely congregated in business and science categories. The level of popularity to a major is positively related to their labor market outcome. This finding reveals the current premarket educational investment pattern for Chinese immigrants and adds to the existing literature by focusing on how detail education quality in terms of major relates to labor market performance.

Main findings

  • In the paper's unweighted descriptive sample, major shares are 35.7 percent engineering, 13.9 percent mathematics and computer science, 12.6 percent business, 10.2 percent physical science, and 8.5 percent biology and life science (Table 5).
  • An omnibus test finds that major composition differs across the Chinese, Japanese, Korean and native-born non-Hispanic white comparison groups. It does not establish a separate tested difference for every pair of groups.
  • Biology and life science, physical science, and mathematics and computer science appear among both the five most popular majors and the five highest fitted employment probabilities. These probabilities depend on the example covariate profile used in the paper (Tables 5, 7 and 11).
  • The employment model uses 3,990 Chinese immigrant men. Separate annual-earnings models use 3,209 Chinese, 699 Japanese, 1,381 Korean and 14,232 native-born non-Hispanic white men, restricted to those who worked more than 40 weeks in the previous year (Table 2).

Data and approach

Data
2009 ACS 1 percent microdata via IPUMS; men aged 25 to 70 with at least a bachelor's degree. The paper groups 38 ACS major categories into 15 fields. Earnings regressions restrict the sample to more than 40 weeks worked in the previous year.
Method
Logit employment models and separate log-linear annual wage-and-salary earnings regressions by group. Employment controls include degree, age, years in the US, English proficiency, family status, citizenship and geography; earnings models additionally include occupation and self-employment.
Contribution
Separates Chinese immigrants from the broader ‘Asian American’ category and links field of study to employment and pay.
Funding
Norman Leonard Research Grant, Department of Economics, Ohio Wesleyan University.

Scope and interpretation

  • Historical 2009 ACS data on men aged 25 to 70 with at least a bachelor's degree. Descriptive shares are unweighted.
  • Employment and earnings regressions are observational. Differences can reflect selection into majors, occupations, education and immigration; the paper does not identify causal returns to changing a major.
  • The earnings outcome is prior-year annual pretax wage and salary income among those who worked more than 40 weeks. It is not an hourly wage or a prediction for today's graduates.

Context

  • The paper separates Chinese immigrants from the broader Asian-American category and distinguishes fields of study rather than considering only years of education.
  • Employment predictions in Table 7 hold characteristics at an example profile. They are not average job-finding probabilities for new graduates.

Further questions

Does the study estimate the causal payoff to choosing a major?

No. It documents historical major choices and conditional employment and annual-earnings associations. The design does not eliminate selection into majors or establish what would happen if an individual changed fields.
