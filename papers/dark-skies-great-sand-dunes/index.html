Contingent Behavior Modeling for Dark Skies Valuation at Great Sand Dunes National Park

Mark Eiswerth, Liam Mullen, Guanyi Yang

American Journal of Agricultural Economics, 2026, pp. 1–25 (accepted 28 April 2026)

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article linked here. Earlier working-paper versions are listed separately below.

Abstract

Dark sky conservation is increasingly popular, requiring facility upgrades and community cooperation. This study assesses its economic benefits at Great Sand Dunes National Park and Preserve, a Gold Tier International Dark Sky Park. Through on-site surveys, we collected data on visitor behaviors, expenditures, and night sky perceptions. We introduced a continuum of contingent night sky brightness levels based on visitors’ home locations and measured changes in visitation. Our Travel Cost-Contingent Behavior analysis shows that each unit increase in nightlight decreases visitation by 0.05 days over 5 years. Using a weighted Latent Class Negative Binomial (LCNB) model, we estimate consumer welfare losses under escalating nightlight pollution scenarios. If the park’s night skies matched an average rural, suburban, or urban area, visitors’ consumer surplus would fall by about 3%, 23–24%, and 42–44%, respectively. These results underscore the substantial economic value of preserving dark skies.

Main findings

  • Under hypothetical increases in park nightlight, the weighted model estimates about 0.05 fewer visitor-days per person over five years for each additional unit of VIIRS radiance, measured in nW/cm²/sr (Table 2).
  • 47 percent of respondents said they would make fewer visits or shorter visits if the park's night sky matched the sky at home. Average visitor-days were 2.68 in the historical state and 1.60 in the contingent state (Table 1).
  • Estimated losses of visitor consumer surplus are about 3 percent under rural brightness, 23 to 24 percent under suburban brightness, and 42 to 44 percent under urban brightness. These ranges compare unweighted and weighted models, not confidence intervals (Table 5).
  • The suburban-brightness scenario implies annual per-person losses of about $39 in the unweighted model and $159 in the weighted model. Scaling those estimates to 500,000 annual visits gives about $19.5 million and $79.3 million. These are modeled recreation use values, not changes in visitor spending or park revenue (Table 5).
  • Consumer surplus per visitor-day is $323.95 in the unweighted latent-class model and $1,226.29 in the weighted model, showing the importance of adjusting for the on-site sampling of frequent visitors (Table 4).

Data and approach

Data
On-site survey of 367 visitor parties at Great Sand Dunes National Park and Preserve, Colorado, on October 20 to 22, 2023; 330 enter the analysis. Home ZIP codes are matched to 2022 cloud-free VIIRS nightlight radiance, and park radiance is measured in October 2023.
Method
Travel-cost and contingent-behavior analysis combining historical visits with responses to a hypothetical home-sky scenario. Fixed-effects negative binomial models estimate nightlight responses; latent-class negative binomial models and inverse visit-day weights estimate recreation consumer surplus while addressing on-site sampling.
Contribution
Develops personally familiar, respondent-specific scenarios for valuing continuous changes in environmental quality, and combines on-site sampling weights with latent-class recreation-demand models.
Funding
State of the Rockies Project and Hulbert Center for Southwest Studies at Colorado College; University of Northern Colorado Department of Economics Foundation Fund. NPS permit GRSA-2023-SCI-0006.

Scope and interpretation

  • The analysis uses 330 visitor-party surveys collected at one park over October 20 to 22, 2023. It does not include nonvisitor existence or bequest values.
  • Future visits are hypothetical responses to a scenario that holds other park attributes constant. The study does not observe a lighting intervention or establish that hypothetical bias is absent.
  • VIIRS upward radiance is not a direct measure of perceived sky brightness and has limited sensitivity to blue-rich LED light. Travel costs exclude the opportunity cost of travel time.
  • Welfare estimates depend on weighting and model assumptions. Aggregate values assume 500,000 annual visits and are not a cost-benefit evaluation of a particular lighting policy.

Context

  • Each visitor compares the park's night sky with the sky where they live. These respondent-specific scenarios create a continuous brightness gradient across the sample.
  • Fixed-effects negative binomial models use historical and contingent visitor-days. Latent-class models estimate travel-cost responses and consumer surplus, with inverse visit-day weighting to address the overrepresentation of frequent visitors.
  • The authors discuss possible applications of personalized scenarios to water quality, wildfire risk and noise. Those domains are proposed applications, not empirical outcomes in this study.

Further questions

How does the study estimate the recreational value of dark skies?

Visitors describe how their trips would change if the park's night sky looked like the sky at home. The authors match those home locations to satellite nightlight and use travel-cost and contingent-behavior models to estimate recreation consumer surplus.

How much would brighter skies affect visitor welfare at Great Sand Dunes?

The model estimates losses of 23 to 24 percent under suburban brightness and 42 to 44 percent under urban brightness. The ranges compare weighted and unweighted specifications. They are hypothetical visitor-use values for this park, not confidence intervals or observed economic losses.

Does the study establish that hypothetical responses are unbiased?

No. Respondents compare the park with a familiar home sky, and the article examines response stability and differences across visitor groups. Those checks do not eliminate the possibility of hypothetical bias.
