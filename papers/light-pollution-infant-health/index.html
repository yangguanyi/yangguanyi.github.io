Artificial Light Exposure During Pregnancy and Infant Health

Andrés Azqueta-Gavaldón (Banco de España); Ella Reese-Clauson (Colorado College); Guanyi Yang (Colorado College)
Working paper · Working paper, Preliminary draft, March 4, 2026
Theme: Economics of the night sky

Using the universe of U.S. births from 2014 to 2018 linked to satellite nighttime radiance, a 10 percent increase in average light exposure during pregnancy reduces birth weight by roughly 0.4 to 2 percent (13 to 65 grams) and raises the probability of NICU admission by about 1 to 3 percentage points, with the largest effects in urban, high-air-pollution areas and among infants born to non-Hispanic Black mothers.

Abstract

Artificial light at night (ALAN) is a pervasive feature of modern environments, yet its effects on fetal development remain poorly understood. Using U.S. birth records linked to satellite-based measures of gestational nighttime radiance, we estimate the impact of prenatal light exposure on infant health. A 10 percent increase in average gestational radiance reduces birth weight by roughly 0.4 to 2 percent (13 to 65 grams at the mean) and increases neonatal intensive care unit admission by about 1 to 3 percentage points. Effects are largest in urban and high air-pollution areas and among infants born to non-Hispanic Black mothers. We estimate these effects using flexible machine-learning methods and complement them with an instrumental-variables strategy that exploits quasi-random variation in the satellite retrieval process to assess attenuation from measurement error.

What this paper shows

  • Sample: 12,027,573 live births, National Vital Statistics System, January 2014–December 2018, linked to county-month VIIRS Black Marble radiance averaged over gestational months.
  • Birth weight: a 10 percent increase in gestational radiance lowers birth weight by 0.84 percent (OLS), about 2.1 percent or roughly 70 grams (double machine learning), and about 4.5 percent (instrumental variables).
  • NICU admission: +0.25 percentage points (OLS), +3.4 percentage points on a 7.4 percent base (double machine learning); the IV estimate is roughly four times the DML estimate.
  • Urban counties −2.4 percent birth weight vs rural −0.9 percent; worst air-quality quartile −2.3 percent vs best quartile −1.5 percent, with a monotonic gradient.
  • By maternal race and ethnicity: non-Hispanic Black −3.25 percent, non-Hispanic White −2.14 percent, Hispanic −1.04 percent, Asian −1.17 percent.
  • The estimates follow a consistent ordering, |OLS| < |DML| < |IV|, which the authors read as evidence that linear models understate the effect.

What it does not show

  • Exposure is county-month outdoor radiance from satellites, not indoor light reaching the mother; residence is measured at delivery. Both forms of error are expected to bias estimates toward zero.
  • The double machine learning estimates rest on selection on observables; the IV estimates identify a local average treatment effect driven by satellite observation quality.
  • No biological mechanism (sleep, circadian disruption) is identified.
  • Preliminary draft; results may change.

Context and definitions

  • “Artificial light at night (ALAN)” is the term used in the health literature; the exposure measure is the VIIRS Black Marble monthly stray-light-corrected composite (VCMSLCFG), in nW/cm²/sr.
  • The instrument is an index of satellite retrieval quality (Black Marble Mandatory Quality Flags), used as a shifter of measurement error, not of true illumination. This differs from Argys, Averett and Yang (2021), who instrument skyglow with Walker’s law.

Data, method, contribution

Data
Restricted-use NVSS natality microdata, all U.S. births 2014–2018 (12.03 million); NASA Black Marble VIIRS monthly radiance and daily quality flags; EPA Air Quality Index; county unemployment, population and income.
Method
OLS with state and year-month fixed effects; double/debiased machine learning (Chernozhukov et al. 2018) with random forests, gradient boosting and lasso, five-fold cross-fitting; instrumental variables using satellite observation quality; standard errors clustered by county × year-month.
What is new
Population-scale evidence that prenatal exposure to artificial light at night worsens birth outcomes, with a measurement-error correction built from the satellite retrieval process itself.
Funding
State of the Rockies Project and Hulbert Center for Southwest Studies, Colorado College.

Questions this paper answers

Does light pollution affect pregnancy and birth outcomes?

In this study, yes. Higher average nighttime radiance over the months of pregnancy is associated with lower birth weight, shorter gestation, more preterm and low-birth-weight births, and more NICU admissions, across OLS, machine-learning and instrumental-variables estimates.

Who is most affected?

Effects are about two and a half times larger in urban than rural counties, larger where air quality is worse, and largest for infants born to non-Hispanic Black mothers, even after conditioning on maternal, economic and environmental characteristics.
