Sibling Spillovers in Rural China: A Story of Sisters

Cynthia Bansak, Xuan Jiang, Guanyi Yang

China Economic Review, Vol. 76, 101873

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article linked here. Earlier working-paper versions are listed separately below.

Abstract

We study sibling spillover effects on the school performance of the elder sibling from the younger sibling using data on multi-children households in rural China. Using a cumulative measure of expected lawful years in school as an instrumental variable, we find a significant increase in school test scores for Chinese language of elder siblings for every year of schooling of the younger sibling. The strongest spillover effects occur when the younger sibling is a girl. Such increases in test scores come from a more intense academic atmosphere within a household when both children enroll in school and are not attributed to differential parental education investments or time inputs. Our findings suggest that policies promoting girls’ education, pre-elementary school age education programs, and after school public resources can have multiplier effects through sibling spillovers.

Main findings

  • The preferred IV estimate is 1.420 additional points on the older sibling's Chinese score per year of younger-sibling schooling, with a standard error of 0.663. It is significant at 5 percent and uses 289 observations. The mathematics estimate is positive but not statistically significant (Table 2).
  • The younger-sister estimate is 2.256 points, significant at 5 percent, in a sample of 103. The younger-brother estimate is 1.251 points and is not statistically significant. These separate significance results do not establish a significant difference between the two groups (Table 4).
  • Estimates vary across the younger-sibling age windows considered. Restricting ages to 5 to 8 gives 4.930 points with 151 observations, so the larger estimate should be read alongside its smaller sample (Table 3).
  • The estimated spillover is about 3.1 points smaller when the older child's school is farther than the median distance from home; this interaction is significant at 10 percent. This is consistent with a household learning mechanism, rather than direct evidence on study time (Table 5).
  • The authors find no statistically detectable schooling-related change in spending on the older child or in parental presence, an imperfect proxy for parental time and attention (Table A3).
  • The first-stage Kleibergen-Paap F statistic is 91.29 in the preferred specification with 289 observations. The baseline specification with 346 observations has an F statistic of 144.66 (Tables 2 and A2).

Data and approach

Data
RUMiC Rural Household Survey, 2008 and 2009 waves, in nine Chinese provinces. The baseline sample is 346 older children in two-child rural households whose younger sibling was aged 3 to 9 in 2008. Chinese and mathematics examination scores are rescaled to 100 points; the preferred regression uses 289 observations.
Method
Two-stage least squares instruments actual years of younger-sibling schooling with cumulative lawful schooling implied by the sixth-birthday school-entry cutoff. Regressions include child, parent, household and geographic controls, with standard errors clustered by city. This is an IV design using school-entry eligibility, not a conventional local regression discontinuity design.
Contribution
Studies education spillovers from younger siblings to older siblings in rural China, with evidence that is strongest for younger sisters and consistent with changes in the household learning environment.

Scope and interpretation

  • The baseline sample has 346 older children from rural two-child households in nine provinces; the preferred specification uses 289. The younger-sister subgroup contains 103 observations.
  • Exam scores are self-reported and rescaled to a 100-point scale. The relevant scores come from one survey wave, and only Chinese-score effects are statistically significant.
  • Identification relies on school-entry eligibility affecting older-sibling scores through the younger child's schooling. The home-environment mechanism is inferred from distance and ancillary regressions, not observed study time.
  • The paper does not evaluate a specific preschool or after-school intervention, long-term attainment, or earnings.

Context

  • The direction of the spillover is younger to elder, the reverse of most of the sibling-spillover literature.
  • The instrument is lawful years of schooling implied by China’s Compulsory Education Law cutoff: children must turn six by August 31 to enroll that September.
  • Earlier versions circulated as “Sibling Spillover in Rural China: A Story of Sisters and Daughters” (IZA Discussion Paper 13127; EdWorkingPaper 21-465).

Further questions

Can a younger sibling's schooling improve an older sibling's performance?

In this rural Chinese sample, the preferred IV estimate is about 1.4 additional Chinese-score points on a 100-point scale per year of younger-sibling schooling. Mathematics effects are not statistically significant, and the finding depends on the school-entry instrument's assumptions.

What does the paper find about younger sisters?

The younger-sister estimate is about 2.26 Chinese-score points and is statistically significant; the younger-brother estimate is imprecise. The authors interpret distance and household-investment results as consistent with a more academic home environment, but do not observe study time directly.
