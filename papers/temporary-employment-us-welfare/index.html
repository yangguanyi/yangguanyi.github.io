Welfare Under Friction and Uncertainty: General Equilibrium Evaluation of Temporary Employment in the U.S.

Guanyi Yang

Research in Economics, Vol. 72, No. 3, pp. 404–413

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article linked here. Earlier working-paper versions are listed separately below.

Abstract

Temporary contracts usually fall outside of employee protection litigation, thus they are often cheaper than permanent contracts and are offered on-demand by firms. In the last two decades, there has been a sharp growth in such contracts in the U.S. labor market. This paper investigates the welfare consequences of offering temporary contracts in the U.S., an environment with low employee protection litigation and high production risk for firms. Employee protection litigation creates firing rigidity in regular labor markets. Pairing firing rigidity with high production risk, firms reduce employment and output, which generates welfare loss. The inexpensive and flexible nature of temporary contracts offers firms a buffer strategy in making employment decisions under risk and navigating the firing rigidity of the regular labor sector, thereby reducing welfare loss. However, temporary contracts cannot fully compensate for the efficiency cost from rising firing rigidity and risk.

Main findings

  • When firing costs rise to one year of wages, normalized net utility is 96.51 with temporary contracts and 95.26 without. Each regime's zero-firing-cost benchmark is normalized separately to 100; these are not consumption-equivalent percentages. (Table 4).
  • Doubling productivity-innovation variance, by raising its standard deviation from 0.259 to 0.3663, yields normalized net utility of 85.66 with temporary contracts and 83.81 without, relative to each regime's lower-risk baseline. (Table 5).
  • Temporary contracts widen the inaction region for regular employment because firms can substitute toward less productive temporary labor when firing costs make regular-worker adjustment expensive.
  • Temporary help accounted for 25.64 percent of job losses in the 2001 recession and 54.99 percent of job gains in the recovery after the 2009 recession. Contributions differ across episodes. (Appendix A).

Data and approach

Data
Longitudinal Business Database firm-size distribution 1977–2013; BLS Current Employment Statistics for temporary help services, 1972–2015; wage ratio and labor share targets.
Method
Dynamic general equilibrium model with heterogeneous firms facing AR(1) idiosyncratic productivity (Tauchen discretization), CES production in regular and temporary labor, a linear firing cost on regular labor, and a representative household with indivisible labor; calibrated to U.S. moments; counterfactuals vary firing costs and volatility with and without temporary contracts.
Contribution
Brings the temporary-contract welfare question to the U.S. setting of low rigidity and high firm risk, with an endogenous temporary-employment share rather than an exogenous one.

Scope and interpretation

  • Annual model period, so within-year temp turnover is not captured; no firm entry or exit; a representative household.
  • Calibrated to the U.S. with a 2 percent temporary share and a 0.7 wage ratio; results are model-based welfare comparisons, not causal estimates.
  • A representative household pools earnings and firm profits, so aggregate welfare results do not establish gains for temporary workers individually.
  • Temporary-help industry employment is a proxy and does not cover all fixed-term, contingent, or gig work.

Context

  • The model extends Hopenhayn and Rogerson (1993) with two labor inputs: regular labor subject to a firing cost and temporary labor hired period by period.
  • The paper argues against treating temporary employment as equivalent to a firing-tax reduction, the interpretation in Aguirregabiria and Alonso-Borrego (2014) for Spain.

Further questions

How do temporary contracts change the aggregate cost of firing restrictions?

They let firms adjust employment around firing costs and partially cushion the aggregate effects of productivity risk. The representative-household model measures aggregate welfare and does not establish whether each temporary worker benefits.
