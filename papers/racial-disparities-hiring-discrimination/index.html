Macroeconomics of Racial Disparities: Discrimination, Labor Market, and Wealth

Srinivasan Murali, Guanyi Yang

Working paper, January 2026 manuscript; arXiv 2412.00615v4 posted 23 April 2026

Read working paper (PDF)Paper DOIarXivBibTeXRIS

January 2026 manuscript. The linked PDF is arXiv version 4, posted 23 April 2026.

Abstract

This paper examines the impact of racial discrimination in hiring on employment, wages, and wealth disparities between black and white workers. Using a labor search-and-matching model with racially prejudiced and non-prejudiced firms, we show that labor market frictions sustain discriminatory practices as an equilibrium outcome. These practices account for 57% of the racial unemployment gap, 48% of the average wage gap, and 16% of the median wealth gap. Discriminatory hiring also increases unemployment and wage volatility for black workers, increasing their labor market risks over the business cycle. Eliminating prejudiced firms reduces these disparities and improves the welfare of black workers as well as the overall economic welfare.

Main findings

  • In the steady-state model counterfactual, removing prejudiced firms narrows the black-white unemployment gap from 7 to 3 percentage points. The mean wage ratio rises from 0.75 to 0.87, the mean wealth ratio from 0.29 to 0.34, and the median wealth ratio from 0.33 to 0.44. These close about 57 percent, 48 percent, 7 percent, and 16 percent of the respective modeled gaps.
  • Quarterly job-finding probability for black workers rises from 0.49 to 0.64 as non-prejudiced vacancies replace exclusive vacancies; white workers' probability falls from 0.66 to 0.64. Racial job-separation rates remain at their benchmark values.
  • Average consumption-equivalent welfare changes are +12.89 percent for black workers, -0.48 percent for white workers, and +1.51 percent overall. Individual welfare changes are averaged using the counterfactual stationary distribution.
  • Prejudiced vacancies have calibrated posting costs of 4.6314 versus 2.5534 for non-prejudiced vacancies, but lower job-destruction rates of 0.0268 versus 0.0644. These type-specific costs and turnover rates allow profitable filled jobs to coexist while free entry drives the expected value of a new vacancy to zero.
  • Eliminating hiring exclusion lowers black unemployment volatility relative to output from 0.26 to 0.18 and the corresponding volatility of the racial unemployment gap from 0.30 to 0.17. The model captures a cyclical mechanism but produces much less unemployment volatility than observed data.

Data and approach

Data
PSID 1996-2014 for the productivity process, sample composition, and wage-cycle evidence; CPS 1996-2014 for unemployment cycles; labor-market flow targets from Cajner et al. (2017); the mean racial wage ratio from Derenoncourt and Montialoux (2021); zero-wealth targets following Nakajima (2021); 2007 ACS and SCF distributions for validation.
Method
Quantitative search-and-matching model with two firm types, Nash bargaining with race-specific bargaining power, unemployment insurance with eligibility, borrowing constraint, race-specific extreme wealth shocks; steady-state counterfactuals and a stochastic equilibrium with TFP shocks.
Contribution
Connects vacancy-level hiring exclusion to unemployment, wage bargaining, wealth accumulation, and business-cycle exposure in a calibrated general-equilibrium search model.

Scope and interpretation

  • The gap shares are conditional model counterfactuals, not independent causal estimates of discrimination's share of observed inequality.
  • The paper models hiring exclusion. It retains calibrated racial differences in job separation, bargaining power, and extreme wealth shocks; it does not model a separate employer rule for wage, promotion, or separation discrimination.
  • The model includes black and white workers and draws on PSID and CPS data for 1996-2014 and additional published calibration targets. It understates the median wealth gap: the modeled black/white ratio is 0.33, versus 0.17 in the cited data.
  • Unemployment volatility relative to output is much too small in the model: 0.26 for black workers and 0.22 for white workers, compared with 9.51 and 11.02 in the data.
  • Welfare results compare stationary equilibria and average individual consumption equivalents. They are not percentage changes in GDP or a transition-path evaluation of a specific anti-discrimination policy.
  • Working paper. This summary was reviewed against the January 2026 manuscript posted as arXiv v4 on 23 April 2026.

Context

  • Firms represent jobs or vacancies. Prejudiced vacancies exclude black applicants by assumption; non-prejudiced vacancies accept both groups. Firms use the same technology, and the model isolates access to opportunities rather than deriving the source of prejudice.
  • The counterfactual removes exclusive vacancies and allows endogenous entry of non-prejudiced vacancies, preserving race-specific benchmark job-separation probabilities. It does not assume that black and white workers become identical along every dimension.
  • Gap shares use modeled baseline denominators. For example, raising the mean black/white wage ratio from 0.75 to 0.87 closes (0.87 - 0.75)/(1 - 0.75), or 48 percent, of the initial wage gap to parity.
  • Welfare is the proportional change in each worker's benchmark consumption stream equivalent to the change in expected lifetime utility. The paper averages these individual changes over the counterfactual stationary distribution.
  • The aggregate-shock extension uses TFP shocks and the Boppart-Krusell-Mitman method based on small-shock transition responses. This is a heterogeneous-agent search model, distinct from the separate HANK race project.

Further questions

How much of the black-white unemployment gap does hiring discrimination explain?

Removing hiring exclusion reduces the model's unemployment gap from seven to three percentage points, about 57 percent. This is a conditional model decomposition that preserves racial differences in job separation, bargaining power, and wealth-shock probabilities.

How can hiring discrimination survive market competition?

Search frictions create valuable worker-firm matches. In this calibrated model, type-specific posting costs and turnover rates permit profitable filled jobs at both exclusive and open firms, even though free entry drives the expected value of a new vacancy to zero.

Can hiring discrimination contribute to the racial wealth gap?

In the model, job-finding risk and wage bargaining affect saving and wealth accumulation. Removing hiring exclusion raises the median black/white wealth ratio from 0.33 to 0.44, closing about 16 percent of the modeled gap to parity; the model understates the observed median wealth gap.

What does the reported welfare gain mean?

The reported +12.89 percent for black workers and +1.51 percent overall are average consumption-equivalent welfare changes between stationary equilibria. They average individual changes using the counterfactual population distribution and do not measure a uniform increase in consumption, income, or GDP.
