Macroeconomics of Racial Disparities: Discrimination, Labor Market, and Wealth

Srinivasan Murali (Indian Institute of Management Bangalore); Guanyi Yang (Colorado College)
Working paper · Working paper, arXiv 2412.00615 (first posted December 2024; revised April 2026). arXiv:2412.00615
Theme: Racial disparities in labor markets and wealth

In a search-and-matching model with prejudiced and non-prejudiced firms calibrated to the U.S., hiring discrimination accounts for about 57 percent of the Black–White unemployment gap, 48 percent of the average wage gap and 16 percent of the median wealth gap; removing it raises Black workers’ welfare by 12.9 percent and aggregate welfare by 1.5 percent.

Abstract

This paper examines the impact of racial discrimination in hiring on employment, wages, and wealth disparities between black and white workers. Using a labor search-and-matching model with racially prejudiced and non-prejudiced firms, we show that labor market frictions sustain discriminatory practices as an equilibrium outcome. These practices account for 57% of the racial unemployment gap, 48% of the average wage gap, and 16% of the median wealth gap. Discriminatory hiring also increases unemployment and wage volatility for black workers, increasing their labor market risks over the business cycle. Eliminating prejudiced firms reduces these disparities and improves the welfare of black workers as well as the overall economic welfare.

What this paper shows

  • Removing prejudiced firms narrows the racial unemployment gap from 7 to 3 percentage points (57 percent of the gap), raises the Black–White mean wage ratio from 0.75 to 0.87 (48 percent of the gap), and raises the median wealth ratio from 0.34 to 0.44 (16 percent of the gap; 7 percent of the mean wealth gap).
  • Black workers’ job-finding rate rises from 0.49 to 0.64 when prejudiced firms exit and non-prejudiced firms enter; white workers’ falls slightly from 0.66 to 0.64.
  • Welfare (consumption equivalents): Black workers +12.9 percent, white workers −0.48 percent, aggregate +1.5 percent.
  • Why discrimination survives: prejudiced firms pay higher vacancy costs (4.63 vs 2.55) but face lower separation rates (0.027 vs 0.064) and earn positive profits, so search frictions let them coexist with non-prejudiced firms.
  • Over the business cycle, discrimination raises Black unemployment volatility (0.26 vs 0.18 without prejudiced firms) and the cyclicality of Black wages (1.08 vs 1.01), and reproduces the countercyclical racial unemployment gap seen in CPS data.

What it does not show

  • Models hiring discrimination only; wage, promotion and separation discrimination are not modeled.
  • Black and white workers only; calibrated to PSID and CPS data for 1996–2014.
  • The model under-predicts the median wealth gap (0.33 vs 0.17 in the data) and, like standard search models, generates too little unemployment volatility.
  • Working paper; the version on arXiv is the current one.

Context and definitions

  • This is a Diamond–Mortensen–Pissarides search model with heterogeneous agents (race, employment status, productivity, assets), not a HANK model; aggregate shocks are solved with the Boppart–Krusell–Mitman sequence-space method.
  • The result that discrimination persists in equilibrium contrasts with Becker (1957), where competition drives prejudiced employers out in a frictionless market.
  • Race-specific bargaining power and extreme wealth shocks are calibrated separately; the appendix quantifies their contribution relative to hiring discrimination.

Data, method, contribution

Data
PSID 1996–2014 (productivity process, zero-wealth shares, wages), CPS 1996–2014 (unemployment by race), labor market flows from Cajner et al. (2017); 2007 ACS and SCF distributions for validation.
Method
Quantitative search-and-matching model with two firm types, Nash bargaining with race-specific bargaining power, unemployment insurance with eligibility, borrowing constraint, race-specific extreme wealth shocks; steady-state counterfactuals and a stochastic equilibrium with TFP shocks.
What is new
A general-equilibrium account of how hiring discrimination is sustained by search frictions and how much of the racial gaps in unemployment, wages and wealth, and in business-cycle exposure, it can explain.

Questions this paper answers

How much of the Black–White unemployment gap is due to hiring discrimination?

About 57 percent in this model: the gap falls from 7 to 3 percentage points when prejudiced firms are removed, with job separation rates held at their calibrated racial values.

Why doesn’t competition eliminate discriminating employers?

Search frictions create rents. Prejudiced firms pay more to fill vacancies but retain workers longer and earn positive profits, so they survive alongside non-prejudiced firms; their competition for white workers also pushes up wages and reduces vacancy posting economy-wide, which hurts Black job seekers.
