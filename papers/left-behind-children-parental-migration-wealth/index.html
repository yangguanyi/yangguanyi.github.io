Does Wealth Matter? An Assessment of China’s Rural-Urban Migration on the Education of Left-Behind Children

Guanyi Yang, Cynthia Bansak

China Economic Review, Vol. 59, 101365

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article linked here. Earlier working-paper versions are listed separately below.

Abstract

This paper decomposes the impact of parental migration on the education of children left behind. In particular, we examine whether children are enrolled in school on a timely basis according to their age when their parents are away. We found both theoretical and empirical evidence to support that parental migration generates a strong positive impact on timely enrollment if a child is from a less wealthy background. However, the effect decreases with family wealth, and reverses after reaching a threshold; we find this point using family house size as our proxy and the turning point occurs at a moderate size of approximately 148 square meters. In addition, we find a compensating effect that migrants tend to spend more on a child’s education investment to offset for the loss of parental time care. Lastly, we found the overall impact of parental migration is negative on the timely enrollment of child. Thus, with the important heterogeneities attributed to wealth, our results suggest that the left behind children of more affluent parents may be pushed into worse human capital outcomes; given the rapid development of China, it may be the case that the current cohort of left behind children is less likely to be enrolled in school than earlier cohorts.

Main findings

  • The household model explains why initial wealth can change the balance between additional financial resources and lost parental time when a parent migrates (Section 3).
  • The empirical model interacts parental migration with house size, a proxy for household wealth. It studies heterogeneous effects rather than one constant migration effect for every family (Equation 1; Tables 2 and 3).
  • The migration-by-house-size coefficient is negative in the lower-wealth subsample. Estimates for wealthier households are less precise, and the overall migration result is sensitive to the controls included (Tables 2, 3 and 6).
  • The authors examine school transportation as an indirect indicator of compensating resource investment. Bus-taking is associated with higher education spending, but these data do not identify school quality or a causal spending mechanism (Tables 4 and 5).

Data and approach

Data
China Health and Nutrition Survey waves from 1997, 2000, 2004, 2006, 2009, 2011 and 2015. The underlying rural sample contains 4,502 children, 2,826 households, 175 communities and 14,298 child-year observations in 12 provinces. Enrollment regressions concern children aged 6 to 18 and use smaller samples because of missing data and age restrictions.
Method
A two-period household model of parental time and financial investment, followed by instrumental-variable regressions of age-appropriate enrollment on migration, house size and their interaction. Instruments use historical community migration networks, provincial wage changes and parental height. Regressions compare wealth subsamples and cluster standard errors by household.
Contribution
Connects a model of parental time and financial resources with evidence on how household wealth can shape the schooling consequences of rural-to-urban migration.

Scope and interpretation

  • House size is a proxy for wealth. The outcome is age-appropriate school enrollment, not examination scores, completed schooling or adult earnings.
  • The 148-square-meter split defines subsamples in this analysis; it should not be read as a universal threshold where migration necessarily changes from beneficial to harmful.
  • Identification relies on migration-network and provincial-wage instruments for migration and parental-height instruments for house size. Exclusion restrictions are assumptions, and several fully controlled first-stage diagnostics are weak.
  • Estimates for wealthier households are imprecise. School transportation is an indirect resource-investment proxy, and its relationship to school quality is not causally established.

Context

  • The theory models child human capital as a product of parental time and financial resources. Migration reduces available parental time but can increase the resources that a household invests in a child.
  • The paper's rural Chinese setting concerns children who remain behind while a parent migrates. It does not estimate school outcomes for children who accompany parents to cities.

Further questions

Why might parental migration affect children's schooling differently across families?

Migration can add financial resources while reducing parental time. The model shows why the balance can depend on initial household wealth, and the empirical analysis examines that heterogeneity using house size as a wealth proxy.

What education outcome does the paper measure?

It measures whether rural children are enrolled in the grade appropriate for their age. It does not measure examination performance or the experience of children who migrate with their parents.

Does the study identify one house-size threshold that applies to every family?

No. It compares subsamples divided at 148 square meters in these data. The results depend on the specification and identifying assumptions, and estimates for wealthier households are imprecise.
