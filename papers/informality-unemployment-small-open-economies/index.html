Unemployment Dynamics and Informality in Small Open Economies

Jaroslav Horvath, Guanyi Yang

European Economic Review, Vol. 141, 103949

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article linked here. Earlier working-paper versions are listed separately below.

Abstract

Despite the typically more pronounced aggregate fluctuations in emerging market economies (EMEs), this paper documents that EMEs exhibit lower relative volatility and countercyclicality of the unemployment rate than small open advanced economies. We link these differences to the larger informal economy in EMEs. We build a small open economy model that combines a formal sector featuring labor search frictions with a frictionless informal sector. A larger informal sector amplifies the impact of productivity and interest rate shocks on formal output, consumption, and employment, while dampening their impact on unemployment. Varying the degree of informality explains a significant fraction of differences in unemployment dynamics across small open economies.

Main findings

  • Unemployment volatility relative to output averages 4.63 in 14 emerging economies and 7.74 in 14 small open advanced economies (p < 0.001). Its correlation with output averages -0.47 and -0.62, respectively (p = 0.047). The quarterly sample spans 1980Q1-2018Q2, with different available periods across countries.
  • Informality averages 30.8 percent of GDP in the emerging group vs 16.4 percent in the advanced group (Schneider et al. 2010); across countries, both relative unemployment volatility and countercyclicality fall as informality rises (p = 0.008 and 0.007).
  • Calibrated to Mexico, the model reproduces relative unemployment volatility of 3.95 (data 3.89) and a correlation of −0.49 (data −0.35), along with consumption more volatile than output and a countercyclical trade balance.
  • Varying the informal employment share from 0.1 to 0.5 moves relative unemployment volatility from 5.82 to 2.78 and the correlation from −0.68 to −0.23, tracing the cross-country pattern.
  • Counting 52 percent of informal output in measured GDP lowers output volatility from 3.24 to 2.58 and raises unemployment volatility relative to output from 3.95 to 4.97. Unemployment itself is unchanged in this measurement experiment.

Data and approach

Data
An unbalanced quarterly panel of 28 small open economies from IMF International Financial Statistics, spanning 1980Q1 to 2018Q2 with different country windows; Schneider, Buehn and Montenegro (2010) informal-output estimates; Mexican labor-market flows from ENEU/ENOE via Leyva and Urrutia (2020); EMBI spreads.
Method
Small open economy real business cycle model with a formal sector subject to search-and-matching frictions and Nash bargaining, a frictionless informal sector with audit probability and fines, CES aggregation of formal and informal consumption, debt-elastic interest rate; log-linearized and calibrated to Mexico; shocks to formal and informal productivity and to the country interest rate.
Contribution
A new cross-country fact about unemployment in emerging markets and a mechanism, the size and mismeasurement of the informal sector, that explains it within a standard search framework.

Scope and interpretation

  • Cross-country facts cover 14 advanced and 14 emerging small open economies with sufficiently long quarterly data.
  • The model is calibrated to Mexico and abstracts from the out-of-labor-force margin; informal production is modeled as firms facing audit risk, not self-employment.
  • Sector-specific productivity shocks are assumed symmetric and uncorrelated, a conservative choice given the lack of data on informal productivity.
  • The cross-country relationships and model counterfactuals do not establish the causal welfare effect of an informality-reduction policy.

Context

  • Informality in the model is the informal share of total employment (0.3 for Mexico); the cross-country figures use the Schneider et al. (2010) share of GDP. The two are different measures.
  • Informal workers search on the job for formal jobs, which is what gives the informal sector its buffering role.

Further questions

How can informal work buffer unemployment fluctuations?

In the calibrated model, expanding formal firms recruit from informal work as well as unemployment. This additional adjustment margin makes unemployment less volatile relative to formal output, providing a mechanism consistent with the cross-country evidence.

Does lower unemployment volatility imply that informality improves welfare?

The paper studies business-cycle dynamics and measurement. It does not estimate the causal welfare effect of formalization or show that workers prefer informal jobs.
