Tourism Response to Dark Sky Conservation at the Great Sand Dunes National Park and Preserve

Mark Eiswerth (University of Northern Colorado); Guanyi Yang (Colorado College); Liam Mullen (Colorado College)
Policy report · State of the Rockies Project, Colorado College, Policy briefing, November 15, 2024. DOI 10.52295/dcc.7959
Theme: Economics of the night sky

Visitors to Great Sand Dunes spend about $80 a day locally and value a day at the park at $324 to $1,226, four to fifteen times their spending; if the park’s sky became as bright as nearby Alamosa, local spending would fall by an estimated $176,000–$191,000 a year and visitors’ net benefits by $0.7–2.9 million a year.

Abstract

No formal abstract is attached to the report; this summary describes it.

This non-technical report presents the findings of an on-site visitor survey at Great Sand Dunes National Park and Preserve, a Gold Tier International Dark Sky Park, conducted October 20–22, 2023. It estimates visitors’ daily spending, the recreation value of a day at the park, and the losses in local tourism revenue and visitor benefits that would follow from increases in night light pollution to the levels of nearby towns and Colorado cities. The scientific analysis is reported in the companion article “Contingent Behavior Modeling for Dark Skies Valuation at Great Sand Dunes National Park”.

What this paper shows

  • 367 visitor parties surveyed; visitors averaged 1.91 trips and 2.68 visitation days per person over the previous five years; 47 percent would reduce trips or days if the park’s sky looked like home.
  • Average spending: $83.20 per trip-day overall and $80.44 per day locally (mostly Alamosa County). Net consumer benefit per day: $324–$1,226.
  • If the park’s night light rose from 0.28 to Alamosa’s 0.76: 0.02 fewer days per visitor over five years, $176,000–$191,000 less local spending a year, about $190,000–$210,000 less regional output (multiplier 1.10), and $708,000–$2.9 million less in visitor benefits a year.
  • At Breckenridge-level light (1.03): $272,000–$295,000 less local spending, $299,000–$325,000 less regional output, $1.1–4.5 million less in visitor benefits a year.
  • At Denver-level light (66.9): 1.8–1.9 fewer days per visitor over five years and $59–232 million a year in lost visitor benefits.

What it does not show

  • Visitors only; no non-visitors or potential future visitors; baseline visitation held at 500,000 a year; direct spending only, with a regional multiplier applied from NPS estimates.

Context and definitions

  • Written for park managers, local officials and the public; methods and updated results are in the AJAE article.
  • The 2022 NPS visitor-spending report puts Great Sand Dunes visitor spending at $32.5 million and local economic output at $36.0 million, which implies the 1.10 multiplier used here.

Data, method, contribution

Data
Same survey as the AJAE article; NPS 2022 visitor spending effects (Flyr and Koontz 2023); VIIRS 2022 nightlights for comparison towns.
Method
Travel cost and contingent behavior models (latent class negative binomial, weighted and unweighted) applied to per-visitor spending and consumer surplus; scaled by annual visitation and a local multiplier.
What is new
Puts dollar figures on local tourism revenue and visitor welfare at risk from light pollution at a specific park, for a non-technical audience.
Funding
State of the Rockies Project and Hulbert Center for Southwest Studies, Colorado College; UNC Department of Economics Foundation Fund.

Questions this paper answers

How much money do dark skies bring to the Great Sand Dunes area?

Visitors spend about $80 a day locally. Even a small increase in light pollution, to the level of nearby Alamosa, is estimated to cost the local area $176,000–$191,000 a year in direct spending, and visitors $0.7–2.9 million a year in lost recreation value.
