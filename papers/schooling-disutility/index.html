How Much Does Schooling Disutility Matter?

Guanyi Yang (Colorado College (St. Lawrence University at publication)); Ben Casner (Federal Trade Commission)
Published article · Research in Economics, Vol. 75, No. 1, pp. 87–95. DOI 10.1016/j.rie.2020.11.004
Theme: College access, human capital and life-cycle risk

Adding a disutility-of-schooling term to a structural education-choice model is equivalent to assuming a relationship between wealth, risk and schooling: with the term present, the utility return to education falls with wealth and, when agents have a precautionary saving motive, rises with the riskiness of future consumption.

Abstract

Studies on structural education choice models are often inconsistent in choosing whether and how to include a disutility of education, especially in an environment with risk and wealth inequality. We show that adding a disutility term to the education decision, a human capital investment option, is equivalent to assuming a relationship between wealth, risk, and education. Utility gain from education is increasing in the riskiness of future consumption. A riskier environment further propels an agent to choose the human capital investment option that maximizes future income. If the degree of risk increases heterogeneously across multiple human capital investment options, risk aversion and the precautionary savings motive can compound or negate each other depending on which option has a greater increase in risk.

What this paper shows

  • Proposition 1: if schooling has a positive financial return and marginal utility is convex (precautionary saving), a mean-preserving spread in future consumption risk raises the utility return to education.
  • Proposition 2: if both schooling and working become riskier but schooling’s risk rises by not too much more, the precautionary-saving effect still dominates risk aversion and schooling becomes more attractive.
  • The utility return to education is decreasing in initial wealth (extending Belley and Lochner 2007 to a risky environment), so the range of human capital for which an agent chooses school narrows with wealth.
  • Implication for estimation: counterfactuals from models that include a disutility term embed these wealth–risk–schooling relations by assumption; models that omit it may understate the role of wealth and risk.

What it does not show

  • A two-period decision model without general equilibrium, bankruptcy, or estimation; disutility is held constant rather than varying with human capital.

Context and definitions

  • The paper is agnostic about whether students actually experience disutility; its point is the consequence of including or excluding the term in structural work.
  • Previously circulated as “The Relationship Between Disutility of Education, Wealth, and Schooling Decisions”.

Data, method, contribution

Data
None (theoretical).
Method
Two-period model of schooling versus work with consumption-saving choice, CRRA utility in the numerical example, mean-preserving spreads following Rothschild and Stiglitz (1970), and envelope-theorem proofs.
What is new
Identifies the implicit assumptions introduced by a schooling disutility term in structural models and adds the precautionary-saving (third-derivative) channel to the risk-and-schooling literature.

Who this is for

  • Structural labor and education economists
  • Quantitative macroeconomists building life-cycle models with human capital
  • Theorists of human capital investment under uncertainty

Questions this paper answers

Does more income risk make people more or less likely to go to school?

With a disutility of schooling and a precautionary saving motive, more risk makes schooling more attractive when it has a positive financial return, because education is a way to store resources for the future. If schooling itself becomes much riskier than working, risk aversion can dominate.
