Better Late Than Never: Macroeconomic Impact of Intermittent College Enrollment and Tuition Subsidies

Guanyi Yang

Journal of Economic Dynamics and Control, Vol. 181, 105219 (available online 21 November 2025)

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article. The download is a smaller PDF with the same text and research figures. The original publisher PDF is also linked below.

Abstract

People often delay starting college or temporarily leave college to work. To examine the welfare implications of intermittent college attendance, I incorporate flexible age-by-age college enrollment choice in a life-cycle model in general equilibrium. College serves as an investment device for the young and reduces risk for the old. Removing flexible access reduces the total welfare value of college by one quarter. Moreover, higher wealth and better human capital preparedness at age 18 incentivize early-age degree completion. However, accessing college at a later age matters more for those who are initially less advantaged. Thus, policies that alleviate financial costs generate considerable long-term welfare improvements.

Main findings

  • In the NLSY79 sample, 9.29 percent complete a bachelor's degree by age 22 and 17.03 percent complete one after a nontraditional path, which includes delayed initial entry as well as interruption. Among graduates, 16.41 percent finish after age 30. NLSY97 and CPS cohorts provide checks on later enrollment patterns (Table 1).
  • Restricting initial enrollment to age 19 and preventing reentry reduces consumption-equivalent welfare by 3.74 percent and bachelor's attainment by 28.48 percent relative to baseline. Removing college entirely reduces welfare by 14.77 percent. The ratio of these welfare losses is about one quarter (Table 6).
  • Removing flexible access produces larger reductions in degree attainment for people with low initial wealth or human capital. The roughly 60 percent decline for the lowest endowment bins is a relative change in BA attainment, not a percentage of all people in those bins.
  • In the calibrated decomposition, the investment value of college peaks at 19 and nearly disappears by 24, while the risk-reduction value emerges after 22 and peaks near 24. These model-specific ages do not establish an empirical cutoff for adult college returns.
  • In a long-run model counterfactual, nationwide free tuition raises BA attainment from 25 to about 78 percent and consumption-equivalent welfare by 39.6 percent, financed by an 8 percent higher lump-sum tax. Exempting part-time student-workers from labor income taxes raises attainment by 16.54 percent relative to baseline and average welfare by 1.62 percent, but the lowest initial-human-capital group loses 2.3 percent after taxes and equilibrium prices adjust.
  • With a market-based borrowing limit, removing flexible access costs 8.57 percent in consumption-equivalent welfare, compared with 9.05 percent from removing college entirely. The importance of flexibility increases under this tighter borrowing assumption.

Data and approach

Data
NLSY79 (1979–2016) for life-cycle enrollment, completion and initial conditions; NLSY97 and CPS ASEC synthetic cohorts for robustness; detrended CPS wages 1992–2016 for calibration.
Method
Overlapping-generations life-cycle model with incomplete markets in general equilibrium (Huggett, Ventura and Yaron 2011 lineage). Each year individuals choose full-time work, part-time work with part-time school, full-time school, or non-employment; human capital accumulates on the job or in college with a sheepskin effect; welfare measured as consumption equivalence (Mukoyama 2010). Model earnings dynamics match Guvenen’s RIP and HIP estimates.
Contribution
Evaluates the welfare value of access to college at each age and separates the investment and risk-reduction roles of college over the life cycle.

Scope and interpretation

  • U.S. data; the NLSY79 follows cohorts born 1957–1964 (the paper checks NLSY97 and CPS synthetic cohorts and argues NLSY79 understates intermittence).
  • The model abstracts from race, gender, geography and industry; a BA is defined as 16 years of completed schooling.
  • Baseline uses a natural debt limit; tighter limits are robustness checks. Shocks take two values to keep computation tractable.
  • Welfare is measured as consumption equivalence, not financial returns to college.
  • Policy results are long-run stationary-equilibrium counterfactuals, not causal evaluations of actual tuition-free programs.
  • Average gains can conceal losses for particular initial-endowment groups.

Context

  • “Intermittent” includes delayed entry after high school, stopout (temporary exit) and re-enrollment, not only dropout.
  • Initial conditions at age 18 are a human capital proxy (AFQT score) and a wealth proxy (average family income at 17–19), fed into the model as an empirical joint distribution rather than a fitted normal.
  • The paper was circulated earlier under the title “Best Time for College? A Tale of Two Endowments”.

Further questions

Why can returning to college help households manage risk?

In the model, people can use college after unfavorable labor-market outcomes, while saving and borrowing also help them adjust. The value of that flexibility depends on age, initial resources and borrowing constraints; it is not an individual recommendation to enroll.

What does the tuition-free counterfactual measure?

It compares long-run stationary equilibria in the calibrated model. Free tuition increases degree attainment and consumption-equivalent welfare under lump-sum financing. Those magnitudes are conditional on the model and financing assumptions, rather than estimates from an implemented program.

Does the student tax exemption benefit every group?

No. Average consumption-equivalent welfare rises by 1.62 percent, but the lowest initial-human-capital group loses 2.3 percent after taxes and equilibrium prices adjust. Changes in enrollment and welfare need to be distinguished (Tables 11 and 12).
