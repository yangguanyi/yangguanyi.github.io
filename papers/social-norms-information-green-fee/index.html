How Much Versus Who: Which Social Norms Information Is More Effective?

Andrew Meyer, Guanyi Yang

Applied Economics, Vol. 48, No. 5, pp. 389–401 (published online 11 September 2015)

Read published paper (PDF)Journal articleBibTeXRIS

Summary based on the published article linked here. Earlier working-paper versions are listed separately below.

Abstract

We conduct an experiment to investigate how different types of information about social norms affect individuals’ stated contributions to a specific pro-environment program, a student ‘green fee’, in the context of a referendum. Compared to students that receive no information about peer contributions, on average, students that receive information about the dollar value range of contributions at peer institutions contribute less while students that learn about the high percentage of students voting ‘yes’ on green fee programs at peer institutions contribute more. The results are economically significant as the absolute values of both effects represent approximately 25% of average contributions. These results suggest that information about participation rates can be more effective than information about dollar amounts in encouraging contributions to environmental initiatives. Of interest to stated preference researchers, we find that results do not change when controlling for self-selection into survey completion.

Main findings

  • In the hypothetical referendum, the control group's mean maximum approved semester fee was $31.83. The fee-amount message reduced it to $23.33, a difference of $8.50; the high-participation message raised it to $38.94, a difference of $7.10 (Table 2).
  • The combined message produced a mean of $32.71, which was not statistically different from the control mean. This does not establish that the two effects cancel exactly (Table 2).
  • The share selecting zero fell from 15.6 percent in the control group to 7.1 percent with the high-participation message (Table 3).
  • Interval-regression estimates were about -$8.09 for fee-amount information and +$7.25 for high-participation information. The latter was significant at 10 percent in this specification (Table 4).
  • Selection models using administrative student data produced similar treatment estimates under the models' assumptions (Table 5).

Data and approach

Data
Online survey of 559 students at one Midwestern liberal arts university in October 2011. Random assignment created control, fee-amount, participation and combined-message groups of 218, 108, 113 and 120. Administrative data for the broader student population support selection-model checks.
Method
Randomized survey information treatments for a hypothetical green-fee referendum. Analysis uses mean comparisons, OLS, maximum-likelihood interval regressions for payment-card responses, and selection models incorporating administrative student data.
Contribution
Compares information about participation with information about contribution amounts in a public-good referendum, and checks the treatment estimates using administrative data on survey participation.

Scope and interpretation

  • The study covers one Midwestern liberal arts university in October 2011, with a response rate of about 30 percent.
  • The outcome is stated support in a hypothetical mandatory-fee referendum. The study does not observe actual payments, voluntary donations or an implemented campus vote.
  • The participation treatment combines information about the number of schools with green fees and high yes-vote percentages. It does not isolate the effect of the number 85 percent or establish that participation information is always more effective.
  • The design cannot distinguish conformity from reciprocity. Similar selection-adjusted estimates do not rule out every form of survey-selection bias.

Context

  • Students were asked for the largest semester fee they would vote to approve if the fee became mandatory for all students. A payment card offered amounts from $0 to $100 in $5 increments.
  • The fee-amount message reported true fees at peer institutions ranging from $1 per credit to $80 per year. The participation message reported programs at more than 70 schools and high approval percentages, including an average of 85 percent.
  • The results compare these specific information messages in a campus referendum setting. Connections to charitable giving or other environmental initiatives are applications to consider, not outcomes observed in the experiment.

Further questions

Which social-norm message increased support for the campus green fee?

In this experiment, the high-participation message raised the maximum fee students said they would approve by about $7 per semester. The fee-amount message reduced it by about $8.50. The experiment tests these messages at one university, not a universal rule about fundraising.

Were these actual contributions?

No. Students selected the largest fee they would support in a hypothetical referendum. If adopted, the fee would apply to all students; actual payments and donations were not observed.
