How Much Versus Who: Which Social Norms Information Is More Effective?

Andrew Meyer (Marquette University); Guanyi Yang (Colorado College (Ohio State University at publication))
Published article · Applied Economics, Vol. 48, No. 5, pp. 389–401 (published online 11 September 2015). DOI 10.1080/00036846.2015.1080803
Theme: Behavior and public goods

In a randomized survey of 559 college students voting on a hypothetical ‘green fee’, telling them that 85 percent of peers at other schools voted yes raised stated contributions by about $7–8 (roughly 25 percent), while telling them the dollar range of peer fees lowered contributions by about $8–9; given both pieces of information, the effects cancelled.

Abstract

We conduct an experiment to investigate how different types of information about social norms affect individuals’ stated contributions to a specific pro-environment program, a student ‘green fee’, in the context of a referendum. Compared to students that receive no information about peer contributions, on average, students that receive information about the dollar value range of contributions at peer institutions contribute less while students that learn about the high percentage of students voting ‘yes’ on green fee programs at peer institutions contribute more. The results are economically significant as the absolute values of both effects represent approximately 25% of average contributions. These results suggest that information about participation rates can be more effective than information about dollar amounts in encouraging contributions to environmental initiatives. Of interest to stated preference researchers, we find that results do not change when controlling for self-selection into survey completion.

What this paper shows

  • Control mean contribution $31.83 per semester. Dollar-range treatment: $23.33 (−$8.50, p = 0.005). Participation-rate treatment: $38.94 (+$7.10, p = 0.035). Combined treatment: $32.71, not different from control.
  • Interval regression estimates: −$8.09 for the dollar-range treatment and +$7.25 for the participation treatment.
  • Zero contributions fell from 15.6 percent of the control group to 7.1 percent in the participation-rate treatment.
  • Heckman and interval selection models using administrative data on all students (GPA, gender, race, region) leave the treatment effects essentially unchanged.
  • Men stated about $10.50 more than women; higher GPA raised contributions; income had no significant effect.

What it does not show

  • One Midwestern liberal arts university, October 2011, about 30 percent response; stated rather than actual contributions in a hypothetical referendum.
  • The design cannot separate conformity from reciprocity motives.

Context and definitions

  • ‘Extensive’ information is how many peers participate (85 percent voted yes at 70+ schools); ‘intensive’ information is how much they pay ($1 per credit to $80 per year). Only true information from AASHE was used.
  • The downward effect of dollar ranges is consistent with generous respondents revising down toward the perceived peer norm.

Data, method, contribution

Data
Online survey of 559 students at a Midwestern liberal arts university, October 2011; four randomized arms; payment card $0–$100 per semester; administrative data on the full student body for the selection model.
Method
Randomized information treatments; t-tests, OLS and maximum-likelihood interval regressions; Heckman selection and Roodman’s cmp interval-selection models.
What is new
Separates the extensive and intensive margins of social-norm information in a referendum setting, and shows that self-selection into a student survey did not change the treatment effects.

Who this is for

  • Behavioral and environmental economists working on social norms and nudges
  • Public-goods and charitable-giving experimentalists
  • Campus sustainability offices and green-fee organizers
  • Stated-preference methodologists

Questions this paper answers

Which social-norm message raises contributions more: how many people give, or how much they give?

How many. Learning that 85 percent of peers voted for a green fee raised stated contributions by about a quarter; learning the dollar range of peer fees lowered them by a similar amount, apparently by pulling generous respondents down toward the norm.
