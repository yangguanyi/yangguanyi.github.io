Economics of the night sky

Light pollution is a continuous, nearly universal environmental change that economics has only begun to price. This line of work measures what dark skies are worth to the people who travel to see them and what artificial light at night costs the people who live under it. The dark-sky work is run through the State of the Rockies Dark Sky Lab at Colorado College with undergraduate researchers.

  • Contingent Behavior Modeling for Dark Skies Valuation at Great Sand Dunes National Park
    Mark Eiswerth, Liam Mullen, Guanyi Yang · American Journal of Agricultural Economics, 2026If the night sky at Great Sand Dunes National Park were as bright as an average suburb, visitors’ consumer surplus would fall by about 23–24 percent; at urban brightness it would fall 42–44 percent. Each unit increase in satellite-measured nightlight cuts visitation by about 0.05 days per visitor over five years.
  • Artificial Light Exposure During Pregnancy and Infant Health
    Andrés Azqueta-Gavaldón, Ella Reese-Clauson, Guanyi Yang · Working paper, 2026Using the universe of U.S. births from 2014 to 2018 linked to satellite nighttime radiance, a 10 percent increase in average light exposure during pregnancy reduces birth weight by roughly 0.4 to 2 percent (13 to 65 grams) and raises the probability of NICU admission by about 1 to 3 percentage points, with the largest effects in urban, high-air-pollution areas and among infants born to non-Hispanic Black mothers.
  • Tourism Response to Dark Sky Conservation at the Great Sand Dunes National Park and Preserve
    Mark Eiswerth, Guanyi Yang, Liam Mullen · State of the Rockies Project, Colorado College, 2024Visitors to Great Sand Dunes spend about $80 a day locally and value a day at the park at $324 to $1,226, four to fifteen times their spending; if the park’s sky became as bright as nearby Alamosa, local spending would fall by an estimated $176,000–$191,000 a year and visitors’ net benefits by $0.7–2.9 million a year.
